One of the central problems with Universal Basic Income (UBI) is that while it provides more money to individuals, it does not increase the supply of goods or services. This imbalance leads to inflation, particularly in sectors where supply is limited and prices are driven by demand. <br><br>To illustrate, consider the pricing model of airline seating. On any given flight, there are only a few first class seats and many more economy seats. First class tickets cost significantly more often $1,200–$2,000 because they are a luxury limited in supply. Economy tickets might cost $200–$400 and are accessible to most travelers. <br><br>Now imagine a policy that grants every person earning below a certain point an extra $1000 per flight . While this sounds beneficial on the surface, it introduces a massive influx of purchasing power without changing the supply of goods or services. With that extra income, many more people would now be able to afford first class seats at least initially. <br><br>The result? A spike in demand without a change in supply. Airlines, seeing increased demand for first-class seats, will raise their prices perhaps to $3,000 or more until only the most willing and able are still purchasing. In other words, prices adjust to absorb the new money in the system. <br><br>Worse, the same effect would ripple through the rest of the economy. Businesses in housing, healthcare, transportation, and groceries would begin raising prices, knowing that consumers have more money. Over time, the purchasing power of the UBI would erode, and the cost of living would rise to match the new baseline. People may be nominally richer, but functionally, they’re back where they started or worse, because inflation can outpace the value of the UBI.
